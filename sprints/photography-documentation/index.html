What counts most
Lesson 7 is your independent evidence: a new object or lighting problem, your own settings. You also need your own original photos and a safe camera and tripod check. Editing someone else’s photo does not show you can use the camera.
Camera rules
  • Strap around your neck or wrist whenever you hold the camera.
  • Never leave a camera on a tripod unattended.
  • Only photograph people who agree, and only for class.
Help you may use

Care and capture workflow
Cameras are expensive and shared. Today you learn the safe routine, set up a tripod properly and take your first baseline photo.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
StepSafe routine
Pick upStrap on first, two hands, lens cap stays on until you shoot
CheckBattery charged, memory card in, lens glass clean (blower or microfibre only; never your finger)
TripodExtend the thickest leg sections first, lock every clamp, keep the centre column low
MountTighten the plate; keep one hand on the camera until it is locked
FinishHold the camera while releasing it; switch off before removing the card; cap on; return it charged
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. In your group, each person completes the full routine while the teacher or a partner observes.
  2. Mount the camera on the tripod and photograph one village object at eye level. This is your baseline.
  3. Copy the file to your folder and name it yourname_baseline.
What you should see: A level, sharp photo of the object, taken from a stable tripod, saved with your name.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Tripod wobbles? Lower the centre column and spread the legs wider.
  • Camera won’t turn on? Check the battery is in and the door is shut.

com11-26-s03-d02Tue 6 Oct · 70 minutes
SLR controls and composition
Automatic mode guesses. Today every person changes one camera control on purpose and frames the object for a reason.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
ControlChangeEffect
Aperture (A/Av mode)f/4 → f/11More of the object is sharp front to back (deeper depth of field)
Shutter speed (S/Tv)1/250 → 1/15 sBrighter, but handheld shots blur. Use the tripod
ISO100 → 1600Brighter, grainier
Focus pointMove it onto the key edgeThe important part is sharp
Produce · 35 minutes
Rotate roles: operator, reviewer, subject handler. Each operator shoots their own set.
  1. Set the mode dial to A/Av. Shoot the object at f/4, f/8 and f/11 from the same spot.
  2. Compose three ways: centred and filling the frame, rule of thirds, and close detail.
  3. Record the settings for each shot on your settings card.
What you should see: Three photos where the amount of the object in focus clearly changes, with settings recorded.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Can’t change the f-number? You may be in Auto (green) mode. Turn the dial to A or Av.
  • Everything dark at f/11? Raise ISO or use a slower shutter on the tripod.

com11-26-s03-d03Wed 7 Oct · 70 minutes
Tripod and viewpoint
The same object can look bigger, smaller or a different shape depending on where the camera is. Documentation needs viewpoints that do not trick the viewer.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
A low viewpoint makes objects look tall and powerful; a high one makes them look small. Very close with a wide lens stretches shapes. For measuring, shoot straight on (front, side, top) from further back and zoom in, with a ruler in frame for scale.
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. On the tripod, photograph the same object from front, side and top (straight on), with a ruler in frame.
  2. Then take one dramatic viewpoint (low or high) for comparison.
  3. Keep the same distance and settings for the three straight-on views.
What you should see: Three matching views with a visible ruler, plus one dramatic view that clearly looks different.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Top view hard? Stand safely on the floor and angle the tripod head down, or place the object on the floor.
  • Ruler unreadable? Move it closer to the object’s front edge and refocus.

com11-26-s03-d04Thu 8 Oct · 70 minutes
Light the object
Light can reveal edges and texture or hide them in shadow. Today you compare two lighting setups for the same object.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
SetupResult
Soft, even (diffused window or lamp through paper)Few shadows: every edge visible; good for documentation
Hard side lightStrong shadows: shows texture and depth, may hide details
Reflector (white card)Fills in shadows on the dark side
Safety: keep lamps away from paper diffusers and fabric; never touch a hot bulb.
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. Setup 1: soft, even light with a white card reflector.
  2. Setup 2: one hard side light.
  3. Same camera position and settings for both, on the tripod.
What you should see: Two photos where you can point to a detail that is visible in one and hidden in the other.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Photo looks orange or blue? Mixed lights. Turn off the room lights, or set white balance to match the lamp.
  • Glare on a shiny part? Move the light to the side, or diffuse it more.

com11-26-s03-d05Fri 9 Oct · 70 minutes
Resolution and file formats
A photo that looks sharp on screen can print blurry. Today you export the same photo for the web and for print, and explain your settings.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
WebPrint
MeasurePixels (e.g. 1600 px wide)Pixels ÷ PPI = inches (e.g. 3000 px ÷ 300 PPI = 10 in)
FormatJPG for photos, PNG for graphics with sharp edges or transparencyHigh-quality JPG or TIFF from the full-size master
Trade-offSmaller files load fastToo few pixels look soft or blocky when printed
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. Open your best documentation photo in Photoshop. Keep the original untouched.
  2. Web export: File › Export › Export As, JPG, 1600 px wide. Note the file size.
  3. Print export: Image › Image Size with Resample unticked, set 300 PPI, and note the print size. Save a high-quality copy.
  4. Compare the two files zoomed to 100%.
What you should see: Two files with different pixel sizes and file sizes, and a print size you calculated.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Print size tiny? The photo may already be small. Export from the original camera file, not a downloaded copy.
  • Export As greyed out? Save the file first or flatten a copy.

com11-26-s03-d06Tue 13 Oct · 70 minutes
Document a making sequence
A process photo story shows each stage of making so someone else could follow it. Consistency is what makes it readable.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
A good sequence keeps the same camera position, light and framing for every stage, so only the object changes. Plan the stages before you start.
Produce · 35 minutes
Open sequence-plan-template.csv. Plan five to eight stages of a village making task (for example, cutting, folding and gluing a building wall).
  1. Lock the tripod and mark its feet with tape.
  2. Photograph each stage from the same spot, with the same light.
  3. Add one close-up of the trickiest step.
What you should see: A set of photos that plays like a flip-book: only the object changes.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Hands in every shot? That is fine for “how”; keep them out of the “result” shots.
  • Stages look the same? Choose stages where something visible changes.

com11-26-s03-d08Thu 15 Oct · 70 minutes
Select and annotate
Now you choose the photos that reveal construction information and label them so a modeller can use them.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
Annotations are short labels and arrows: a dimension, a material, a joint type. Keep the original photo on its own layer and add labels on separate layers so the original is not changed.
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. Choose 3–4 photos: front, side, top and one detail.
  2. Add labels: overall dimensions (from your ruler), material, and one joint or opening.
  3. Mark clearly which images are original captures and which have edits.
What you should see: A reference sheet where someone could find the height, width and one construction detail without asking you.
Stuck? Try this:
  • Labels hard to read? Use a solid box behind the text.
  • Too many labels? Keep only what a modeller needs.

Defend and hand over to modelling
You explain your lighting and viewpoint choices, and package the photos and dimensions the modelling sprint will need.
Retrieve · 5 minutes
Demonstrate and predict · 10 minutes
Sprint 4 builds digital models and technical drawings. Front, side and top photos with a scale become your reference views.
Produce · 35 minutes
  1. Defence (2 minutes with your teacher): show your settings record and your best documentation photo. Explain your light and viewpoint.
  2. Package: a folder with your front/side/top views, reference sheet and exports.
  3. Write the modelling reference brief: the views provided, key dimensions and anything missing.
What you should see: A tidy, named folder another student could use to start modelling.

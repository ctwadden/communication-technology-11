A logo is a visual identifier: a name, symbol, or combination that helps you recognise an organisation. Colour, shape and lettering are choices you can examine.

A brand is bigger than a logo. Packaging, websites, advertising and people's experiences also influence what they associate with an organisation.

For today's task, ask: What can I see? What does it suggest to me? What does the source actually say? The LEGO lettering and Amazon packaging in Step 3 give you two examples to consider.

How did the logo get that way?

Open your logo's source below. Read just the relevant part. Find one useful fact about an idea, a design change, or a reason behind the logo.

LEGO · a redesign that focused on the name

LEGO's official history says its 1973 redesign kept the square but removed “system” and five coloured stripes to focus attention on the company name. The designers tightened the rounded lettering and added black/yellow outlines around the white letters.

Source: LEGO Group — “A modern, international company” ↗

On the page, find the heading “New logo”. This note is a summary of that section.

Amazon · turning an idea into a symbol

Design studio Turner Duckworth explains that Amazon wanted to move beyond selling books. Its designers made a curved smile-arrow linking A to Z, representing the expanded range. They also placed the smile on shipping boxes.

Source: Turner Duckworth — “Amazon: Everything from A–Z” ↗

Read the opening paragraphs. This is the design studio's account of its own work.

6. Who might it connect with?

Suggest one possible audience. Name an interest or need, then explain one logo feature that might appeal to them.

Start here: “It might connect with people who ... because the logo ...”

Write this answer in your Google Doc.

Audience = people a message may reach. Interests and needs matter; do not assume a colour is “for” a particular gender or that everyone of one age likes the same design. NOVA users: suggest a possible fictional client or audience.

7. Change one thing
